# voice.md (DRAFT v2, built from 1 reference video; not yet approved by the user)

The channel's narrator voice. Read by every yt-* skill in this repo. Template:
`youtube/voice.template.md`.

Reference: a ~10 min "what if you were reborn with all your memories" story video the user picked
as the model. Only the patterns below are taken from it. Never reuse its lines, characters or plot.

## Who I am talking to

One person who loves a "what would actually happen?" story and will stay for ten minutes if it
keeps being funny and keeps making them feel something. They are the main character: the whole
video happens to "you".

## How I actually talk

- **Second person, present tense, always.** "You wake up." "You're six." Never "imagine if" or
  "let's say": the viewer is already inside the scenario.
- **Short sentences and fragments.** Most under 12 words. One-word lines are allowed and used for
  impact ("Heartburn.").
- **The narrator plus characters.** Narration carries the story; short spoken lines from 2 to 4
  recurring characters (and "you" thinking out loud) carry the jokes and the feeling. Dialogue is
  2 to 8 words per line, plain and natural.
- **Time stamps as scene changes.** Each scene starts with an age, a year or a time ("At four.",
  "2008.", "9:41 p.m."). That is the scene cut.
- **Concrete numbers over adjectives.** Prices, counts, dates, times. "11 views" beats "barely
  anyone watched".
- **Deflating jokes.** A big claim, then a flat, ordinary reply that undercuts it. Roughly one
  laugh every 20 to 30 seconds.

## The structure every episode follows

1. **Cold open (0:00-0:20).** Drop the viewer into a vivid, specific moment with something at
   stake. Plant one mystery the video will pay off ("one thing in particular"). No title card, no
   intro.
2. **The rules (first 1 to 2 minutes).** Show what the scenario means physically and practically,
   with one or two real facts.
3. **The fun part.** "You" use the premise and it works, with escalating payoffs and jokes.
4. **The setback.** Something fails, and a real-world rule explains why (a science fact or a
   real-world limit). The viewer learns how this world works.
5. **The real goal.** The fun premise turns out to be hiding an emotional one. Set it up early,
   mention it in passing, pay it off in the last third.
6. **The climax.** The planted mystery pays off, under time pressure, with a real fact that raises
   the stakes.
7. **Bookend.** The last scene echoes the first one, changed.
8. **Playful coda, then one question.** A quick light beat, then ask the viewer what they would do
   in this scenario, and to subscribe. One sentence.

## Devices to reuse (as techniques, with new content every time)

- **Running gags:** a catchphrase from a side character, repeated 3 to 5 times, with the last one
  flipped.
- **Callbacks:** a line from the first third comes back in the last third meaning something new.
- **A real fact every 45 to 90 seconds**, stated flat, then tied straight back to "you". Facts are
  the "most likely what would happen" part of the channel.
- **Never tag a fact with "That's real", "Real fact:" or "by the way, that's true"** (the user's
  rule, 2026-11-12). Name the source in the sentence instead: "A 2018 Cornell study found...",
  "Studies show...", "Researchers found...". The on-screen fact card carries the source.
- **An object that carries the plot:** a list, a note, a number the viewer keeps watching.
- **Music cues** at emotional turns. Silence right before the biggest moment.

## Words I never use

"Imagine if", "let's say", "smash that subscribe button", "without further ado", "let's dive in",
"in today's video", "mind-blowing", "insane". No narrator talking about the video itself.

## Words I do use

Plain spoken English. Contractions everywhere. Lines that are funny because they are said flat.

## What I will not claim

- Every real fact in a script is checkable. Script drafts list the source for each fact in a
  note. If a fact cannot be sourced, cut it or say it as speculation.
- The scenario is fiction, but everything that happens because of it follows real-world logic.

## My format

- Long-form, 16:9, about 8 to 10 minutes (about 1,300 to 1,600 spoken words). Shorts get cut from
  it later with `/yt-shorts`.
- No face on camera. Simple flat-color animations, one idea per scene, a simple recurring cast.
- Voices: one narrator plus a distinct voice per recurring character.
